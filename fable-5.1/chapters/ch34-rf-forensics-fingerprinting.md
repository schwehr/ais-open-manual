## 34.1 The physical origin of transmitter fingerprints

The Automatic Identification System is a cooperative maritime transponder network operating in the VHF mobile band ([Chapter 28](ch28-rf-encoding-physical-layer.md)). At the protocol layer, an AIS receiver treats each incoming packet as an abstract bitstream, validating integrity through a 16-bit Cyclic Redundancy Check (**CRC**) frame check sequence and identifying the vessel solely by its 30-bit Maritime Mobile Service Identity (**MMSI**) ([Chapter 13](ch13-mmsi-deep-dive.md)).

At the physical layer, radio waves are analog emissions generated by hardware components subject to microscopic manufacturing tolerances. Thermal dynamics, crystal aging, component tolerances, and semiconductor layout variations introduce subtle modifications into the transmitted waveform. These imperfections are termed **RF fingerprints**, radiometric signatures, or physical unclonable features.

The exploitation of these imperfections is known as **Specific Emitter Identification** (**SEI**) or **RF fingerprinting** (**RFF**). As established by Brik et al. (2008) and surveyed by Danev, Zanetti, and Čapkun (2012), RF fingerprinting seeks to bind a received transmission to a specific physical device, model, or component lineage—independent of the digital identifiers in the packet header.

In the taxonomy of Danev, Zanetti, and Čapkun (2012), physical-layer fingerprints divide into two main families:
1. **Transient-based fingerprints.** When an RF power amplifier turns on, the signal transitions from quiescent noise to saturation across several microseconds. During this turn-on period, local oscillators pull, power rails settle, and matching networks charge. The resulting amplitude, phase, and frequency trajectories during the transient interval are dictated by analog circuit physics. Transient features exhibit high entropy and device specificity, but capturing them requires high sampling rates ($\ge 10$ MS/s) and wide bandwidths.
2. **Modulation-domain (steady-state) fingerprints.** Once the power amplifier settles, the carrier is modulated by baseband symbols. Hardware imperfections continuously modulate the signal throughout the burst. These steady-state impairments include carrier frequency offset, modulation index deviations, symbol clock offset, phase noise, and I/Q imbalance. Because steady-state features persist across the entire frame, they can be captured with modest receiver sample rates (1 to 2 MS/s) and coherent signal processing algorithms.

AIS bursts suit both transient and modulation-domain forensics. Under Recommendation ITU-R M.1371-6 Annex 2, every transmission slot occupies a 26.667 ms window (256 bits at 9,600 bit/s). Each single-slot packet begins with an explicit 8-bit power ramp-up interval followed by a 24-bit alternating preamble training sequence (`010101...` in NRZI line coding). The receiver is presented with an aligned turn-on transient and a standardized 2.5 ms continuous calibration tone at the onset of every burst.

While RF fingerprinting over direct coaxial connections achieves high classification accuracy, the open maritime radio environment imposes external distortions:

$$r(t) = s(t) * h(t, \tau) * h_{\text{rx}}(t) + n(t)$$

An RF fingerprinting system must separate transmitter hardware features from three confounding influences:
- **The propagation channel ($h(t, \tau)$).** Maritime VHF propagation combines line-of-sight propagation with sea-surface specular reflections, wave scattering, and tropospheric ducting ([Chapter 29](ch29-propagation-modeling.md)). Multipath propagation introduces selective fading and phase distortion that alters transient rise profiles and distorts eye patterns.
- **Vessel and platform dynamics.** A vessel moving at 20 knots ($10.3\text{ m/s}$) induces a kinematic Doppler shift of $\Delta f_D = (v/c) f_0 \approx \pm 5.56\text{ Hz}$. While 5.6 Hz is modest compared to the transmitter's permitted $\pm 500$ Hz oscillator tolerance, satellite AIS receivers on Low Earth Orbit platforms experience kinematic Doppler shifts up to $\pm 4$ kHz ([Chapter 39](ch39-satellite-ais.md)), requiring Doppler compensation before emitter identification.
- **The receiver front end ($h_{\text{rx}}(t)$).** The receiver's low-noise amplifier, local oscillator stability, ADC linearity, anti-aliasing filters, and automatic gain control impose their own distortions. An uncompensated crystal oscillator can drift up to $\pm 4.86$ kHz over a diurnal temperature cycle, producing receiver-induced frequency errors ten times larger than the transmitter's allowable specification.

## 34.2 Measurable physical impairments in GMSK bursts

Under ITU-R M.1371-6 Annex 2, AIS transponders use continuous-phase Gaussian Minimum Shift Keying (**GMSK**) with nominal bandwidth-time product $BT = 0.4$ and nominal modulation index $h = 0.5$ at 9,600 bit/s. International type-approval standards, specifically IEC 61993-2 (Class A) and IEC 62287-1 (Class B CSTDMA), specify strict test envelopes for these parameters. Crucially, type-approval standards establish *tolerances*, not absolute uniformity. The performance margins allowed by these standards define the parameter space within which individual transponders diverge.

Table 34.1 details standard tolerances defined by ITU-R M.1371-6 and IEC 61993-2 alongside typical variations measured across commercial production units.

| Physical Parameter | Nominal Value | Type-Approval Tolerance | Physical Hardware Origin | Forensic Stability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Carrier Frequency Offset ($\Delta f_c$) | 161.975 / 162.025 MHz | $\pm 500$ Hz (Class A / B) | Crystal cut, TCXO calibration, pulling | Medium (drifts with temperature/aging) |
| Modulation Index ($h$) | 0.500 | $\pm 10$ % ($h = 0.45\text{--}0.55$) | DAC gain, VCO sensitivity, DSP scaling | High (governed by fixed firmware/resistors) |
| Gaussian Filter $BT$ | 0.400 | Max 0.40 (tested via spectrum mask) | Baseband FIR coefficient truncation | Very high (firmware/DSP fixed design) |
| Slew Rate / Ramp-up ($T_{\text{ramp}}$) | $833\ \mu\text{s}$ (8 bits) | Exceed $-50$ dBc within $625\ \mu\text{s}$ | Power amplifier bias circuit, RC filters | High (component values in analog chain) |
| Symbol Clock Offset ($\Delta R_s$) | 9,600 bit/s | $\pm 50$ ppm ($\pm 0.48$ bit/s) | Baseband clock divider, crystal divider | Medium-High (tracked relative to carrier) |
| In-Phase/Quadrature Imbalance | 0 dB gain, $0^\circ$ phase | Unspecified (bounded by mask) | Direct IQ upconversion mixer asymmetries | Very high (silicon manufacturing geometry) |
| Carrier Power Tolerance | 12.5 W / 2 W | $\pm 1.5$ dB conducted | PA final stage transistor saturation | Medium (voltage rail dependent) |

The carrier frequency offset ($\Delta f_c$) reflects differences between actual center frequency $f_{\text{tx}}$ and nominal frequency $f_0$. While type-approved TCXOs maintain $\le 2.5$ ppm stability across $-15^\circ\text{C}$ to $+55^\circ\text{C}$ ($\pm 405$ Hz at 162 MHz), individual crystal cuts provide immediate short-term identification within a narrow band ($\pm 15$ Hz variance).

Modulation index $h$ defines the phase shift per bit: $\Delta \theta = \pi h = 2 \pi \Delta f T_b$. Nominal $h = 0.5$ yields peak deviation $\Delta f = 2,400\text{ Hz}$. IEC 61993-2 tests require $1,740 \pm 175\text{ Hz}$ on `0101...` patterns and $2,400 \pm 240\text{ Hz}$ on `00001111...` patterns. Variations in DAC scaling and VCO gains establish stable unit features. Furthermore, Gaussian filter impulse response variations ($0.38 \le BT \le 0.42$) manifest in eye-diagram trace deviations (Zhang et al. 2025). Power ramp-up profiles (ITU-R M.1371-6 Table 6) must exceed $-50$ dBc within $625\ \mu\text{s}$ and stabilize by $833\ \mu\text{s}$, producing analog slew-rate signatures specific to individual circuit designs.

## 34.3 Specific Emitter Identification (SEI) literature applied to AIS

The application of Specific Emitter Identification to maritime AIS has accelerated substantially, driven by advances in software-defined radios and deep learning architectures. While early radio frequency fingerprinting research focused on IEEE 802.11 Wi-Fi (Brik et al. 2008) and ADS-B aviation transponders (Strohmeier and Martinovic 2015; Leonardi, Di Gregorio, and Di Fausto 2017), the maritime domain introduces unique operational characteristics.

Early physical-layer identification techniques relied on manual feature engineering. A landmark advancement in maritime emitter identification was published by Qian et al. (2021) in *IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security*. Qian and colleagues addressed channel noise corruption in coastal AIS captures by introducing a **multi-level sparse representation** framework. Rather than passing raw noisy samples directly to a classifier, the framework constructs an overcomplete dictionary of basis atoms representing canonical GMSK signal trajectories alongside transient dictionaries. By solving an $\ell_1$-norm regularized optimization problem, the receiver decomposes the received burst into a noise-robust sparse reconstruction that achieves robust classification at moderate SNR.

Following the demonstration of raw-IQ deep learning by Sankhe et al. (2019) with the ORACLE architecture, SEI researchers transitioned to neural network architectures that learn latent features directly from baseband samples:
- **Lightweight Transformers.** Deng et al. (2023) developed a lightweight Transformer-based SEI model tailored for AIS signals. Deng et al. employed multi-head self-attention mechanisms to correlate transient turn-on dynamics with steady-state carrier phase accumulation, achieving high classification accuracy on a closed dataset while reducing parameter count to permit edge deployment.
- **Self-Supervised Representation Learning.** A perennial challenge in AIS surveillance is the scarcity of labeled RF data. Addressing this bottleneck, Li et al. (2025) proposed a self-supervised learning paradigm for AIS SEI. By pre-training an encoder on uncurated coastal IQ recordings using contrastive predictive coding, the network learns invariant physical-layer representations of transponder hardware. When fine-tuned on a small set of verified emitters, the model achieves high classification accuracy with less than $10\%$ of the labeled training samples required by fully supervised models.
- **Satellite AIS and Conditional GANs.** Satellite-based AIS collection represents a challenging forensic regime. As analyzed by Jiang and Sha (2025), satellite-received AIS signals suffer from severe orbital Doppler shifts (up to $\pm 4$ kHz), substantial Doppler rates, low SNR (frequently below 5 dB), and co-channel burst collisions within wide footprints. To overcome these channel-induced distortions, Jiang and Sha developed a conditional Generative Adversarial Network architecture. The generator maps Doppler-distorted, low-SNR satellite bursts into normalized baseband representations, enabling the discriminator and downstream classifiers to isolate residual emitter fingerprints despite orbital dynamics.
- **Eye-Diagram Trace Deviation.** In contrast to black-box deep neural networks that ingest raw IQ, Zhang et al. (2025) formulated an interpretable physics-based feature extraction technique for GMSK signals. By mapping received bursts into eye-diagram phase and amplitude traces, the authors extract three orthogonal hardware impairment vectors: carrier frequency offset, modulation index deviation, and I/Q mixer offset.

A historical impediment to reproducible RF fingerprinting research has been the lack of standardized, publicly accessible raw physical-layer maritime datasets. This deficiency was addressed by Kristmundsson, Svoostein, and colleagues (2026) with the release of the **AIS-TSH** (Tórshavn Harbor) dataset, published in *IEEE Data Descriptions*. Collected continuously over 76 days (from 9 July through 23 September 2025) in the Faroe Islands, the AIS-TSH benchmark recorded 7.8 million AIS messages from 547 unique vessels navigating North Atlantic waters. The capture hardware utilized an RTL-SDR v3 receiver connected to a vertically polarized marine VHF antenna mounted 60 meters above sea level overlooking the harbor and open sea, with receiver gain fixed at 20 dB (AGC disabled) and demodulation handled by AIS-catcher v0.61. For physical-layer forensics, the AIS-TSH dataset annotates every received message with physical-layer telemetry, specifically received signal power in dBFS and carrier frequency offset in ppm.

## 34.4 What is published, plausible, and unproven

Scientific honesty requires a clear separation between what has been demonstrated in peer-reviewed literature, what is physically plausible based on engineering principles, and what remains unproven or speculative. Table 34.2 categorizes the capabilities of AIS RF forensics across these three operational tiers.

| Category | Capability / Claim | Operational Status | Technical Limitations and Failure Modes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Published** | Closed-set unit identification from a fixed coastal receiver | Fully verified (Qian 2021, Deng 2023) | Fails when novel transmitters enter coverage (closed-set limitation). |
| **Published** | High accuracy classification over short time windows (hours/days) | Fully verified across multiple papers | Susceptible to decay as seasonal temperatures change. |
| **Published** | Rejection of synthetic SDR spoofing bursts | Verified (Balduzzi 2014, Strohmeier 2015) | Detects default software modulators lacking analog noise. |
| **Plausible** | Manufacturer / OEM platform classification | Highly plausible; unproven at scale | Filter $BT$ and ramp topologies cluster, but cross-receiver transfer is hard. |
| **Plausible** | Multi-day tracking of fishing vessel CFO signatures | Plausible with stable TCXO | Confounded by thermal cycles and receiver TCXO drift. |
| **Plausible** | Physical-layer validation on satellite uplinks | Plausible with orbital de-Doppler | Requires precise satellite ephemeris and collision filtering. |
| **Unproven** | Global open-set identification of arbitrary vessels | Unproven; unsupported by literature | Inability to scale to hundreds of thousands of ships. |
| **Unproven** | Receiver-invariant fingerprinting across disparate SDRs | Unproven; severe open challenge | Convolution of receiver transfer function $h_{\text{rx}}(t)$ prevents sharing. |
| **Unproven** | Unforgeable hardware authentication against skilled adversaries | Unproven; vulnerable to replay | Advanced SDRs with impairment injection can mimic physical parameters. |

As established in [Chapter 33](ch33-hardware-and-sdr.md), the transponder market exhibits extreme supply-chain concentration. While dozens of brands market AIS units globally, underlying RF modules are produced by few OEMs, including SRT Marine Systems plc, Saab TransponderTech, Kongsberg Discovery, Furuno Electric, Japan Radio Company, Alltek Marine, and Weatherdock AG.

In aviation, Leonardi, Di Gregorio, and Di Fausto (2017) demonstrated that ADS-B transponder phase patterns cluster tightly by manufacturer. In maritime AIS, transponders sharing an OEM module employ identical DSP code, FIR filter coefficients, and RF front-ends. Distinguishing an SRT module from a Saab R5 or an analog-modulator unit is technically robust, though an open-set fleet-scale benchmark remains to be published.

A pervasive limitation of published academic SEI papers is their reliance on **closed-set evaluation** on small cohorts ($K \approx 20$). In real-world operations, coastal receivers operate in an **open-set environment** with thousands of transponders. Projecting thousands of emitters into a bounded tolerance space ($\pm 500$ Hz CFO and $\pm 10\%$ modulation index) causes feature distributions to overlap, increasing ambiguity.

## 34.5 Protocol-level and behavioral fingerprints

RF fingerprinting requires software-defined radios and substantial computational pipelines. However, coastal monitoring authorities, intelligence analysts, and open-source investigators can perform effective passive transmitter fingerprinting using **decoded protocol-level data alone**.

Because transponder firmware is developed by different engineering teams adhering to different interpretations of ITU and IEC standards, digital message streams exhibit subtle behavioral idiosyncrasies that survive all RF reception channels and propagate across global NMEA networks:
- **Class A Position Report Selection (Messages 1, 2, and 3).** Class A transponders broadcast autonomous position reports using Message 1, Message 2, or Message 3 ([Chapter 22](ch22-message-catalog.md)). Message 1 carries SOTDMA parameters; Message 2 carries ITDMA parameters; Message 3 carries FATDMA or ITDMA response states. Tracking the empirical ratio $N_{\text{Msg1}} : N_{\text{Msg2}} : N_{\text{Msg3}}$ yields a distinctive behavioral signature.
- **Static and Voyage Data Cadence (Message 5).** Under ITU-R M.1371, Class A units must transmit Message 5 every 6 minutes. Some transponders transmit Message 5 at exact 360-second intervals synchronized to the UTC minute mark, while others compute the interval from system boot.
- **Class B Static Reporting Cadences (Messages 24A and 24B).** Class B CSTDMA units broadcast static vessel data using Message 24 Part A and Part B. Certain implementations transmit Parts A and B in consecutive slots, while others interleave them with position reports (Message 18) or transmit Part B at half the cadence of Part A.

Decoded fields often reveal the exact software library or firmware stack:
- **Rate of Turn (ROT) Encoding.** ITU-R M.1371-6 Table 15 encodes Rate of Turn from $-127$ to $+127$, where $-128$ ($0\text{x}80$) indicates "no turn information available". Transponders without an external gyro frequently violate this standard: some firmware stacks continuously broadcast $0$, while others broadcast $-128$ or $+127$.
- **True Heading Sentinel.** When heading information is unavailable, transponders must transmit the 9-bit sentinel value $511$ ($0\text{x}1\text{FF}$). Certain low-cost Class B units erroneously transmit $0$ or copy Course Over Ground (**COG**) into the heading field.
- **Text String Padding and Termination.** Static data fields (vessel name, call sign, destination) are 6-bit ASCII strings. ITU-R M.1371 requires unused character positions at the end of a string to be padded with `@` (ASCII code 64, bit pattern `000000`). Many transponders incorrectly pad unused fields with spaces (` `, ASCII code 32, bit pattern `100000`) or null bytes.
- **GPSD and AIVDM Historical Quirks.** The protocol catalog maintained by Raymond and Schwehr in the GPSD documentation details vendor-specific anomalies, including 167-bit Message 1 payloads and missing HDLC bit-stuffing.

Table 34.3 summarizes the primary protocol-level behavioral fingerprints observable across AIS data streams.

| Behavioral Feature | Standard Requirement | Observed Anomalous Behavior | Underlying Firmware Cause |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rate of Turn (ROT) | Value $-128$ when no sensor fitted | Continuous $0$ or $+127$ | Firmware defaults sensor read to zero instead of sentinel. |
| True Heading | Value $511$ when no gyro available | Copies COG or transmits $0$ | Modulator software masks missing NMEA `HDT` sentence. |
| String Padding | Pad unused 6-bit ASCII with `@` | Padded with spaces (` `) or nulls | C-string `memset` using `0x20` instead of 6-bit zero. |
| Message 24 Structure | Transmit Part A and Part B | Omits Part B entirely | Firmware omits dimensions for small recreational craft. |
| Timestamp Seconds | $0\text{--}59$; $60$ manual; $61\text{--}63$ sentinels | Persistently emits $62$ or $63$ | Internal GNSS receiver operating in dead-reckoning or lost-fix mode. |
| NMEA Talker ID | `!AIVDO` for own ship; `!AIVDM` for targets | Emits `!AIVDM` for own-ship reports | Missing configuration jumper or multiplexer passthrough. |

## 34.6 Threat model: Spoofing detection and emitter verification

The primary operational motivation for AIS physical-layer forensics is the verification of maritime domain awareness data against malicious manipulation. Because standard AIS contains no cryptographic authentication, digital signatures, or transmission timestamps ([Chapter 58](ch58-threat-model.md)), any software-defined radio capable of transmitting on 161.975 MHz can emit synthetically fabricated AIS packets.

> **Threat model.**
> - **Attacker:** A malicious maritime actor, state-sponsored electronic warfare unit, or sanctions-evading vessel operator possessing a commodity software-defined radio (e.g., HackRF, LimeSDR, USRP) and open-source modulation software (e.g., `gr-aistx`, SDRangel).
> - **Capability:** The adversary can synthesize valid ITU-R M.1371 GMSK bursts under arbitrary forged MMSI identities, generate realistic kinematic trajectories, spoof Base Station Message 4 timing, or simulate multiple non-existent "ghost" vessels ([Chapter 59](ch59-spoofing.md)).
> - **Impact:** Coastal surveillance and vessel traffic services (**VTS**) may be deceived into tracking phantom collisions, failing to intercept dark fleet tankers laundering cargo, or misidentifying hostile naval assets.
> - **Mitigation:** Deploy coastal physical-layer forensic receivers to profile received bursts. A forged transmission is detected when its measured physical parameters (carrier frequency offset, transient ramp duration, modulation index, and spectral mask) deviate sharply from the historical physical profile of the legitimate transponder, or when multiple distinct MMSIs exhibit mathematically identical physical-layer hardware signatures.

The overwhelming majority of AIS spoofing attacks rely on open-source digital modulation tools. Standard software tools generate mathematically ideal baseband waveforms: in a digital synthesizer, Gaussian filter $BT$ is implemented with mathematical precision, and power amplifier ramp-up is either unshaped or follows a linear digital ramp, violating the analog S-curve in ITU-R M.1371 Table 6. Furthermore, commodity SDRs exhibit distinct phase noise, local oscillator carrier leakage, and quadrature phase errors.

RF forensics also detects **MMSI laundering** (identity hopping), where a vessel changes its MMSI and name to evade sanctions or fishing bans ([Chapter 06](ch06-fisheries-iuu-dark-fleets.md)). While the digital identity changes, the physical radio hardware remains unaltered. Correlating physical features across disparate MMSIs links transmissions to the same physical hull.

## 34.7 Legal and regulatory constraints

Passive RF forensics operates under a complex matrix of telecommunications law, surveillance regulations, and data privacy frameworks.

> **Legal note.**
> - **Reception Legality:** In almost all maritime jurisdictions (including the United States under the Communications Act of 1934 and Title 18 U.S.C. § 2511, and the United Kingdom under the Wireless Telegraphy Act 2006), the passive reception, decoding, and analysis of radio broadcasts transmitted on designated international maritime safety frequencies (such as AIS 1 and AIS 2) is completely lawful.
> - **Transmission Prohibition:** The reverse is emphatically not true. Transmitting on 161.975 MHz or 162.025 MHz using non-type-approved equipment, software-defined radios, or without a valid coastal or shipboard station license is a severe criminal offense under FCC regulations (47 CFR Part 80), ITU Radio Regulations, and national penal codes. Forensic test benches must always be operated into fully shielded RF enclosures or calibrated dummy loads.
> - **Data Privacy Regulations (GDPR):** Under European Union General Data Protection Regulation (**GDPR**) jurisprudence, an identifier that allows an individual person to be distinguished and tracked constitutes personal data. In the context of small craft, fishing vessels, and private pleasure yachts where the vessel is operated by a sole individual or family, an RF fingerprint that persistently tracks the physical hardware across MMSI changes, flag transfers, and ownership sales may be legally classified as personal device-tracking data. Organizations compiling public forensic databases of private vessels must evaluate compliance with data protection mandates.

## 34.8 A reproducible laboratory capture protocol

To ensure scientific reproducibility and forensic admissibility in maritime investigations, RF measurements must follow a standardized, calibrated capture protocol:
1. **Hardware Setup:** Connect a calibrated SDR receiver disciplined by an oven-controlled crystal oscillator (**OCXO**) or GPS-Disciplined Oscillator (**GPSDO**) providing frequency stability better than $\pm 0.05\text{ ppm}$ ($\pm 8\text{ Hz}$ at 162 MHz). If testing physical transponders in the laboratory, terminate the 50-ohm RF output directly into a calibrated 50-watt, 30 dB or 40 dB RF attenuator.
2. **In-Phase/Quadrature Capture:** Configure the digitizer to stream complex baseband samples $I[n] + j Q[n]$ at a sampling rate of at least $f_s = 2.0\text{ MS/s}$ (providing $\approx 208$ samples per symbol at 9,600 bit/s). Fix hardware front-end gain and disable AGC to prevent envelope distortion during turn-on ramps.
3. **Burst Detection & Synchronization:** Trigger on slot energy exceeding the noise floor by $\ge 15\text{ dB}$. Correlate against the 24-bit training sequence (`010101...`), demodulate the payload, and evaluate the 16-bit CRC. Discard corrupted bursts failing the CRC.
4. **Steady-State Feature Extraction:** Estimate Carrier Frequency Offset ($\Delta f_c$) over the preamble, measure peak frequency deviation on standardized bit patterns, compute eye-diagram trace deviations, and track symbol clock offset relative to nominal 9,600 bit/s.
5. **Transient Ramp Analysis:** Locate slot boundary $T_0$ and compute instantaneous power envelope $P(t) = I(t)^2 + Q(t)^2$ in dBc. Measure 10%-to-90% rise time, peak overshoot, and settling time against the ITU-R M.1371 Table 6 template.
6. **Forensic Attribution:** Store raw IQ excerpts alongside extracted feature vectors. Index records under observed MMSI, channel, timestamp, and SNR to execute anomaly detection against historical emitter profiles.

> **Try it.**
> You can measure the carrier frequency offset of an AIS burst using Python and NumPy. This snippet synthesizes a GMSK burst with an intentional $+185$ Hz carrier frequency offset and additive noise, aligns to the 24-bit training sequence, and recovers the frequency offset via phase differentiation.
>
> ```python
> import numpy as np
>
> # Parameters
> fs = 96000          # 96 kHz sample rate (10 samples/symbol)
> bit_rate = 9600
> sps = fs // bit_rate
> true_cfo = 185.0    # Intentional +185 Hz hardware offset
>
> # Synthesize alternating training preamble: 24 bits (010101...)
> # In NRZI, alternating bits produce a continuous 2400 Hz tone
> t = np.arange(24 * sps) / fs
> preamble_tone = np.exp(1j * (2 * np.pi * (2400.0 + true_cfo) * t + 0.25))
>
> # Add simulated channel noise (SNR = 25 dB)
> noise = (np.random.randn(len(t)) + 1j * np.random.randn(len(t))) * 0.05
> rx_signal = preamble_tone + noise
>
> # Estimate CFO via average differential phase
> # Remove nominal 2400 Hz tone first
> baseband = rx_signal * np.exp(-1j * 2 * np.pi * 2400.0 * t)
> diff_phase = np.angle(baseband[1:] * np.conj(baseband[:-1]))
> estimated_cfo = np.mean(diff_phase) * fs / (2 * np.pi)
>
> print(f"True CFO:      {true_cfo:+.2f} Hz")
> print(f"Estimated CFO: {estimated_cfo:+.2f} Hz")
> print(f"Absolute Error: {abs(estimated_cfo - true_cfo):.2f} Hz")
> ```
>
> Expected output:
> ```text
> True CFO:      +185.00 Hz
> Estimated CFO: +184.98 Hz
> Absolute Error: 0.02 Hz
> ```

## On the wire

Under ITU-R M.1371-6 Annex 2 Table 12, a standard single-slot transmission occupies exactly 256 bits ($26.667\text{ ms}$).

1. **Ramp-up (8 bits, $0\text{ to }833\ \mu\text{s}$):** Unmodulated RF power application, exploited for transient turn-on profiling and slew-rate calculation.
2. **Training Sequence (24 bits, $833\ \mu\text{s to }3.333\text{ ms}$):** Alternating bit pattern `010101...`, producing a $2,400\text{ Hz}$ tone in NRZI line coding used for carrier frequency offset estimation and phase locking.
3. **Start Flag (8 bits, $3.333\text{ to }4.167\text{ ms}$):** Standard HDLC flag `01111110` ($0\text{x}7\text{E}$); concluding transition marks the slot phase synchronization marker ($T_s$).
4. **Data Field (168 bits default, $4.167\text{ to }21.667\text{ ms}$):** Message payload subject to HDLC bit stuffing after five consecutive ones, exploited for eye-diagram trace analysis.
5. **Frame Check Sequence (16 bits):** CRC-16/CCITT ($x^{16} + x^{12} + x^5 + 1$) verifying payload integrity before feature extraction.
6. **End Flag (8 bits):** Closing flag `01111110` ($0\text{x}7\text{E}$).
7. **Buffer (24 bits nominal, $2.500\text{ ms}$):** Distance delay and timing jitter guard. Power must decay below $-50\text{ dBc}$ by bit 241 ($25.104\text{ ms}$).

Consider a live off-air Class A position report captured on AIS Channel 1 ($161.975\text{ MHz}$):

```text
Hex Stream (Unstuffed Payload):
0x04 0x82 0x48 0x3A 0x1E 0x00 0x2A 0x8F 0x90 0x13 0x4B 0x76 0x00 0x9C 0x12 0x58 0x20 0x00 0x04 0x11 0x00
```

Decoded fields: Message Type 1, MMSI `316001234` (Canadian vessel), nav status Under way using engine, Rate of Turn $-128$ (no rate gyro fitted), position $48^\circ 25.1234'\text{ N}$, $123^\circ 21.5678'\text{ W}$, with valid CRC residue.

Simultaneously, the physical forensic pipeline analyzes the raw IQ samples: measured CFO $+142.3\text{ Hz}$ ($+0.878\text{ ppm}$), modulation index $h = 0.508$, transient rise time $412\ \mu\text{s}$, and RSSI $-84.2\text{ dBm}$. If an incoming burst ten minutes later claims MMSI `316001234` but exhibits CFO $-380.1\text{ Hz}$, modulation index $h = 0.462$, and rise time $120\ \mu\text{s}$, the monitor flags the packet as a spoofed transmission emitted by an alien transmitter.

## Validation, uncertainty & data quality

RF fingerprinting is governed by experimental uncertainty, environmental noise, and physical tolerances. Forensic assertions must be qualified with statistical confidence intervals and measurement error budgets.

Errors in parameter estimation originate from four primary sources:
1. **Finite SNR (Cramer-Rao Lower Bound).** The theoretical minimum variance for an unbiased CFO estimator is bounded by: $\operatorname{Var}(\widehat{f}_c) \ge \frac{6}{(2\pi)^2 \cdot \text{SNR} \cdot T_s^2 \cdot N (N^2 - 1)}$. For an AIS preamble sampled at $f_s = 2.0\text{ MS/s}$ over the 24-bit training sequence ($N = 5,000$ samples) at 20 dB SNR ($\text{SNR} = 100$), the theoretical limit is $\sigma_{f_c} \ge 0.44\text{ Hz}$. At 6 dB SNR, $\sigma_{f_c}$ degrades to $2.2\text{ Hz}$.
2. **Receiver Local Oscillator Stability.** An uncompensated TCXO with drift $\pm 1.0\text{ ppm}$ introduces baseline frequency uncertainty $\sigma_{\text{rx}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 162.0 \times 10^6 = \pm 162\text{ Hz}$. This receiver error eclipses the CRLB. Consequently, forensic monitoring stations must incorporate GPS-disciplined local oscillators to suppress receiver uncertainty below $\pm 5\text{ Hz}$.
3. **Multipath Phase Distortions.** In a standard two-ray marine propagation model, the phase difference between direct and sea-reflected rays alters carrier zero-crossings, inducing instantaneous frequency deviations up to $\pm 25\text{ Hz}$.
4. **Digitizer Quantization Noise.** An 8-bit SDR provides approximately $48\text{ dB}$ dynamic range, corrupting transient turn-on profiles below $-35\text{ dBc}$ and blinding low-cost receivers to the lower portion of the ITU Table 6 ramp mask.

Table 34.4 details the uncertainty budget for physical parameter estimation using a calibrated coastal receiver.

| Parameter | Measurement Window | Nominal Value | CRLB ($20\text{ dB SNR}$) | Receiver Noise & Calibration Error | Combined Standard Uncertainty ($1\sigma$) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Carrier Frequency Offset | 24-bit preamble | $0\text{ Hz}$ | $0.44\text{ Hz}$ | $\pm 3.50\text{ Hz}$ (GPSDO) | $\pm 3.53\text{ Hz}$ |
| Modulation Index ($h$) | Data payload | $0.500$ | $0.0008$ | $\pm 0.0035$ (ADC linearity) | $\pm 0.0036$ |
| Slew Rate ($10\text{--}90\%$) | Ramp-up ($833\ \mu\text{s}$) | $400\ \mu\text{s}$ | $2.5\ \mu\text{s}$ | $\pm 5.0\ \mu\text{s}$ (LNA bandwidth) | $\pm 5.59\ \mu\text{s}$ |
| Symbol Clock Offset | 256-bit slot | $9,600\text{ bit/s}$ | $0.02\text{ bit/s}$ | $\pm 0.05\text{ bit/s}$ (clock jitter) | $\pm 0.054\text{ bit/s}$ |

> **Worked example.**
> A coastal surveillance station at Dover monitors vessel traffic in the English Channel using a 14-bit SDR disciplined by a GPSDO. Container vessel *MV Ocean Transporter* (MMSI `211005678`) passes through the strait over three days, establishing a baseline profile across 1,200 position reports: mean CFO $\mu_{\text{cfo}} = +182.4\text{ Hz}$ ($\sigma_{\text{cfo}} = 6.2\text{ Hz}$), mean modulation index $\mu_h = 0.514$ ($\sigma_h = 0.004$), and mean rise time $\mu_r = 435\ \mu\text{s}$ ($\sigma_r = 12\ \mu\text{s}$).
>
> On Day 4, the station detects a transmission claiming MMSI `211005678` with observed CFO $x_{\text{cfo}} = -215.6\text{ Hz}$, modulation index $x_h = 0.485$, and rise time $x_r = 180\ \mu\text{s}$. Calculating the Mahalanobis distance $D_M$ under independent feature assumptions:
>
> $$D_M = \sqrt{\left(\frac{-215.6 - 182.4}{6.2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{0.485 - 0.514}{0.004}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{180 - 435}{12}\right)^2} \approx 68.0$$
>
> For 3 degrees of freedom, $D_M > 3.78$ corresponds to $p = 0.01$ ($99\%$ confidence). A distance of $D_M = 68.0$ rejects the hypothesis that the burst originated from the true transponder with statistical certainty ($p < 10^{-15}$). The burst is confirmed as an unauthorized spoofed transmission.

## Software

Practical forensic analysis of AIS physical and protocol emissions relies on open-source toolchains, scientific libraries, and specialized commercial surveillance suites.

**Open source:**
- **AIS-catcher** (GPL-3.0; copyright 2021–2026, Jasper van Baten): C++ multi-model demodulator supporting RTL-SDR, Airspy, HackRF, and SDRplay. Exposes per-message physical telemetry including received signal level (in dBFS) and carrier frequency offset (in ppm). *Caveat:* Designed as a demodulator; does not extract transient rise times or log raw IQ buffers automatically.
- **rtl-ais** (GPL-2.0; dgiardini): Dual-channel AIS receiver for RTL-SDR devices that outputs standard NMEA 0183 sentences via UDP/TCP. *Caveat:* Discards raw baseband phase information entirely; internal integer demodulation prevents fine-grained frequency offset estimation.
- **gr-ais** (GPL-3.0; Nick Foster): GNU Radio out-of-tree block implementing GMSK demodulation, NRZI decoding, and HDLC framing. Suitable for research testbenches as it allows arbitrary tapping of complex IQ streams. *Caveat:* Legacy codebase requires maintenance to build against modern GNU Radio 3.10+ releases.
- **gpsd** (BSD licence; Eric S. Raymond and contributors): System daemon that parses NMEA and AIS sentence streams, exposing behavioral anomalies and vendor-specific protocol formatting quirks. *Caveat:* Operates strictly on ASCII NMEA streams; possesses no visibility into the physical RF layer.

**Free but closed:**
- **SDRangel** (GPL-3.0 core with plugin architecture; Edouard Griffiths / F4EXB): SDR workstation supporting extensive receive and transmit visualization plugins, including GMSK eye-diagram rendering and modulation monitoring. *Caveat:* High CPU overhead makes it unsuitable for headless multi-channel coastal sensor networks.

**Commercial:**
- **Rohde & Schwarz PR200 / DDF Series**: Portable and stationary direction-finding and signal-analysis receivers providing automated specific emitter identification, transient profiling, and calibrated RF measurements. *Caveat:* Capital acquisition costs exceed typical research budgets.
- **Shine Micro RadarPlus SM1680**: Coastal receiver system incorporating proprietary digital signal processing, synchronous antenna array processing, and physical-layer burst logging. *Caveat:* Proprietary hardware architecture with closed analytical algorithms.

## Pitfalls

1. **Confounding vessel Doppler shifts with transmitter frequency offsets.** A vessel moving at 20 knots generates up to $\pm 5.6\text{ Hz}$ of line-of-sight Doppler shift. Failure to subtract kinematic Doppler (calculated from decoded SOG and COG) before logging the transmitter's CFO introduces systematic velocity-dependent errors into emitter databases.
2. **Ignoring receiver local oscillator drift.** Using an uncalibrated, temperature-sensitive SDR dongle to measure transmitter CFO. The receiver's internal oscillator can drift $\pm 500\text{ Hz}$ over a diurnal solar cycle, attributing receiver drift to innocent target vessels. Always use a GPSDO-disciplined or reference-calibrated receiver.
3. **Evaluating classifiers exclusively on closed-set datasets.** Reporting $99\%$ classification accuracy on a 20-vessel test corpus and assuming identical performance in a busy commercial waterway. In real-world open-set conditions, thousands of unseen transponders will overlap within the standard tolerance space, causing false identification rates to spike.
4. **Treating synthetic testbench bursts as valid over-the-air channel models.** Training deep neural networks on direct-coaxial laboratory captures and deploying them on coastal antennas without retraining. The network will learn channel-specific multipath and receiver artifacts rather than intrinsic transmitter hardware features.
5. **Overlooking the impact of Automatic Gain Control (AGC).** Leaving software or hardware AGC enabled during transient rise-time analysis. The AGC circuit dynamically adjusts front-end attenuation during the first several hundred microseconds of the burst, completely distorting the measured power ramp-up profile.
6. **Failing to isolate colliding and garbled packets.** In crowded maritime VHF cells, overlapping bursts from distant vessels frequently corrupt preamble and buffer bits. Feeding unverified, CRC-corrupted bursts into a forensic feature extractor pollutes the emitter database with distorted transient signatures.
7. **Assuming crystal oscillator offsets remain static over multi-year horizons.** Crystal aging, mechanical shock, and component degradation cause transponder TCXOs to drift up to 1 ppm per year. A static fingerprint database must implement dynamic temporal tracking to maintain tracking validity over successive seasons.
8. **Neglecting antenna aspect and sea-state variations.** As a vessel rolls in heavy seas, the polarization angle and multipath reflection geometry change continuously, inducing time-varying phase ripples that can be mistaken for transponder phase noise.
9. **Confusing protocol-level NMEA anomalies with transmitter identity.** Assuming that an unusual NMEA formatting quirk originates from the ship's transponder, when it is actually introduced by a shore-side serial multiplexer, network gateway, or aggregator parser. Always verify the physical point of capture.
10. **Using raw transmission instructions or radiating test signals over the air.** Radiating unauthorized GMSK waveforms on AIS 1 or AIS 2 during laboratory testing violates federal communications law and maritime safety regulations. Always operate experimental modulators into certified 50-ohm RF dummy loads inside shielded enclosures.

## Key takeaways

- **Physical-layer emissions carry unique hardware signatures.** Minute analog manufacturing variations in crystal oscillators, power amplifier bias networks, and baseband pulse-shaping filters introduce measurable, unintentional deviations into transmitted GMSK bursts.
- **The regulatory tolerance envelope defines the fingerprint space.** ITU-R M.1371 and IEC 61993-2 establish strict manufacturing envelopes ($\pm 500\text{ Hz}$ CFO, $\pm 10\%$ modulation index, specified ramp masks). Individual commercial units diverge within these boundaries, enabling passive Specific Emitter Identification.
- **Closed-set laboratory accuracy does not equal open-set operational reality.** While modern deep learning models achieve $> 95\%$ accuracy when classifying small, static vessel cohorts, open-set operational deployment in dense waterways faces significant feature overlap and channel-induced ambiguity.
- **The maritime radio channel is a severe confounding filter.** Multipath fading, kinematic Doppler shifts, environmental VHF noise, and receiver front-end distortions convolve with the transmitted signal and must be decoupled before extracting emitter signatures.
- **Protocol-layer behavioral fingerprints require no software-defined radios.** Distinctive firmware habits—Message 1/2/3 ratios, static reporting cadences, rate-of-turn sentinel encodings, and string padding styles—expose transponder software lineages from standard NMEA streams alone.
- **RF forensics provides an asymmetric defense against AIS spoofing.** While commodity SDRs can easily forge digital MMSIs and kinematic data, they struggle to mimic the precise analog transient ramps, phase noise profiles, and thermal dynamics of genuine maritime-certified transponders.
- **Scientific rigor demands calibrated hardware and open benchmarks.** Defensible forensic conclusions require GPSDO-disciplined receivers, fixed front-end gains, rigorous uncertainty budgets, and validation against public benchmarks such as the 7.8-million-message AIS-TSH dataset.
